## What is an illustrative tattoo?

Illustrative is the widest lane in tattooing. It borrows from pen-and-ink drawing, children's books, scientific plates, comics and watercolor, and it doesn't commit to one rulebook the way traditional or realism do. The tell is intent: every mark is there because the picture needs it, not because a style demands it.

In practice that means an illustrative piece might be a stippled sparrow on a maple branch with no color at all, or a blue tit sitting on an ink sketch over a red watercolor splash, drips and all. Both are illustrative. What they share is drawing, first and last.

## How does Chris approach illustrative work?

He treats the skin like a page. Chris likes fun, surreal images with an animated, whimsical feel, so his illustrative pieces tend to have a twist: a paw print assembled from pieces of a husky's face, an origami penguin folded from patterned paper, a raven in a plague doctor's hat and goggles. Playful, but drawn seriously.

He's just as comfortable without color. The alligator surfacing through the cattails and the roses-and-monarch half sleeve are both black-and-grey, built on soft shading instead of heavy outline. When you send your idea, mention whether you're picturing color, black-and-grey, or you're not sure yet. Not sure is a fine answer.

## What subjects suit an illustrative tattoo?

Pets, wildlife, botanicals, characters you grew up with, objects with a story attached, and anything that combines two of those. Illustrative handles memorial pieces well because it can hold a likeness without going full photo-realism. It also suits humor: Snoopy as an embroidered patch, or a Fraggle waving from a gilded frame.

Bring the real thing when you can: the photo of your dog, the actual leaf, the record sleeve. A tattoo drawn from your reference will always feel more like yours than one built from a stock image.

## Where does illustrative work sit best on the body?

Wherever the drawing has room to be a drawing. Forearms and calves suit a single subject with a background wash; upper arms and thighs give a full scene space to breathe. Fine detail and soft shading want a flat, low-friction area, so hands, fingers and feet are worth a conversation before you commit.

Size follows detail. A portrait needs more inches than a silhouette, and watercolor effects read best when they have somewhere to spread. If you're unsure, tell Chris the largest size you'd be comfortable with and let the drawing find its own size inside that.
