## What makes a tattoo neo-traditional?

Look for a heavy outline that thins where it needs to, color that's saturated but shaded rather than flat, and a subject drawn with a little theater in it. Traditional keeps things to a handful of colors and a fixed set of images. Neo-traditional keeps the readability and drops the rulebook.

The style grew out of artists who loved the punch of old flash sheets but wanted to draw filigree, fur, glass and light. You still get the thing that makes traditional tattoos age well, a strong black skeleton holding everything together, with a lot more room to play inside it.

## What subjects work best in this style?

Anything with a strong silhouette: animals, birds, botanicals, hands, candles, ships, portraits of pets, objects that mean something to you. Neo-traditional loves a subject it can wrap ornament around. It is also a good home for ideas that are a little odd, like a snake with its spine showing or a fish behaving like a flower.

If you bring two ideas that don't obviously belong together, say so. Combining elements is where Chris does some of his best work, and a first draft that's slightly too weird is easier to dial back than one that's playing it safe.

## How big should a neo-traditional tattoo be?

Bigger than you think, and smaller than you fear. The style needs room for the outline to breathe and for shading to sit inside it, so a palm-sized piece is a comfortable minimum for a single subject. Forearms, upper arms, calves and thighs are the sweet spot; hands and necks are a conversation.

Shrinking a neo-traditional design too far means dropping the details that made you want it. If you're set on a small placement, a simpler subject with one or two colors will hold up better over the years than a busy one squeezed down. You'll get a straight answer if a size isn't going to work.
